PSLE Science · P5–P6 · Marking

How PSLE Science papers are marked, and why your child loses marks even when they know the concept.

Marks go to specific ideas written in the right words. Here is what the marker looks for and the four ways correct understanding still loses marks.

Your child can explain a concept at the dining table, yet loses marks on the same topic in the paper. The reason is that PSLE Science marks are given for specific ideas written in the right words, not for general understanding, so an answer that is "roughly right" often earns nothing. Once you see what the marker is looking for, the lost marks become much easier to find and fix.

Multiple-choice and open-ended questions test different things

The PSLE Science paper has a multiple-choice section (Booklet A) and a section of structured and open-ended questions (Booklet B). In Booklet A the answer is either right or wrong, so the marks lost are mostly about misreading the question or being fooled by a close option. In Booklet B, the child must write the answer in their own words, and this is where most marks leak, because the marker compares what is written against a list of expected ideas.

The marker is ticking ideas off a list

A mark scheme for an open-ended question is, in simple terms, a list of key ideas. Each idea that appears in the answer, in acceptable science language, earns a mark. This has three practical consequences:

  • Keywords matter. Terms such as conduction, good conductor, poor conductor, evaporation and condensation are the ideas the marker is looking for. Everyday phrases like "the heat goes through" are usually too vague to match.
  • Each mark needs its own idea. A 2-mark question needs two distinct points. Saying the same thing twice in different words still counts as one.
  • The answer must link to the question. Writing everything the child knows about the topic does not earn marks if it does not answer what was asked.

Four ways a correct idea still loses marks

  • Describing instead of explaining. "The spoon becomes hot" restates the question. The marker wants the reason.
  • Missing the keyword. The idea is in the child's head but written in everyday words.
  • Stopping one step early. The cause is stated but not connected to the result, so the chain of reasoning is incomplete.
  • Adding a wrong statement. A correct point sitting next to a contradicting one can lose the mark.

Example: the same idea, two different mark outcomes

Question (2 marks): A metal spoon is left in a bowl of hot soup. After a few minutes, the handle of the spoon feels warm. Explain why.

Weak answer: "The heat from the soup goes up the spoon so the handle is hot." The child understands what happens, but there is no keyword and no property of the metal, so the marker has little to tick.

Full-marks answer: "Metal is a good conductor of heat. Heat is transferred from the hot soup, along the spoon, to the handle by conduction." Two ideas, each in the expected language, each linked to the question.

What to do with your child's marked script

Take one marked Booklet B paper and, for every question with missing marks, ask a single question: "Which idea did the marker want that is not written here?" Sort each lost mark into one of the four leaks above. Within a few questions a pattern usually appears. One child keeps skipping the keyword, another keeps describing instead of explaining. A pattern is far easier to correct than a vague feeling of "careless".

PSLE Science marking: common questions

Does a longer answer get more marks in PSLE Science? +

No. Marks are given for the specific ideas the question asks for, not for length. A long answer that circles the topic can earn nothing, while a short answer with the right keyword and the link to the question earns full marks. Extra statements that are wrong or contradict the correct idea can also cost marks.

Do I lose marks if my child uses everyday words instead of the science keyword? +

Often, yes. Mark schemes look for the science idea in correct terms, such as conduction, good conductor, evaporation or condensation. Everyday words like "the heat goes in" or "the water disappears" are usually too vague to match. Teach the keyword together with the concept.

How many points should a 2-mark open-ended answer have? +

As a safe rule, plan for two distinct points when a question is worth two marks, and make sure each one connects back to what the question asked. Read the marks beside the question as a guide to how much the answer needs.
