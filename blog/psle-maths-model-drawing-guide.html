Model drawing for PSLE Maths: how to turn a word problem into a picture your child can solve.

A bar model is a quick sketch that shows who has what and how the amounts compare. Here is the 4-step method we use, with a worked example.

If your child stares at a word problem and does not know where to start, model drawing is the first skill to build. A bar model turns the words into rectangles, so the relationship between the amounts is visible before any calculation begins. It does not replace Maths knowledge; it gives the knowledge somewhere to go.

Why a model works when the sentence does not

Word problems hide the structure inside the language: "3 times as many", "2 more than", "the rest". Children who rush into numbers often add when they should divide, or use the wrong total. Drawing first forces them to decide what is being compared, and that decision is where most wrong answers begin.

The 4-step model drawing method

  1. Read once for the story, once for the ask. Underline the question first, such as "How many does Ali have?", so the unknown is clear.
  2. Draw one bar for each person or item. Use equal-sized parts for the smaller quantity and repeat that part for the bigger one.
  3. Label every number from the question on its bar. Put a bracket and the total where it belongs, and a question mark on the unknown.
  4. Turn the model into working. Count the units, find the value of 1 unit, then answer the question. Write it line by line with a unit and a statement.

Where a model goes wrong

  • Unequal parts drawn as equal. If two bars are meant to be the same size but are drawn differently, the model misleads the child.
  • Unknown on the wrong bar. The question mark must sit on what the question asks for, not on the first thing mentioned.
  • Missing labels. A drawing with no numbers is only a picture. Every figure from the question should appear in the model.
  • Stopping at the drawing. The model is a plan. The working and the final statement still earn the marks.

Example: "times as many" with a total

Question: Ali and Ben have 72 stickers altogether. Ali has 3 times as many stickers as Ben. How many stickers does Ali have?

Model: Draw one box for Ben and label it 1 unit. Draw three boxes of the same size for Ali. Put a bracket over all the boxes and write 72.

Working: Total units = 1 + 3 = 4 units
4 units = 72
1 unit = 72 ÷ 4 = 18
Ali: 3 units = 3 × 18 = 54

Statement: Ali has 54 stickers.

Check: Ben has 18, and 54 + 18 = 72. Ali's 54 is 3 times 18. Both conditions hold.

Model drawing: common questions

Does my child have to draw a model to get full marks? +

No. The marking is about correct method and a correct answer, not a particular picture. But for many word problems a bar model is the clearest way to show the method, so the working is easy for the marker to follow and easy for your child to check.

When should my child use a model instead of algebra-style working? +

At the primary level, bar models are the standard way to handle 'more than', 'times as many', total and difference problems. If a question gives a total and a relationship between two quantities, a model is usually the quickest route.

Why does my child's model look right but the answer is still wrong? +

Usually the units are not lined up: bars of different lengths are labelled as the same amount, or the unknown is marked on the wrong bar. Check that equal-sized parts are drawn the same length and that the question mark sits on what the question actually asks for.

How neat does the model need to be? +

Neat enough that every bar and label can be read. Drawing bars with a ruler is not required, but equal parts should look roughly equal, and every number from the question should appear next to the bar it belongs to.
