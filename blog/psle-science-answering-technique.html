PSLE Science answering technique: the 3-part open-ended answer that earns the marks.

Say what happens, give the keyword reason, link it to the question. Here is how to build each part, with a worked example.

The best PSLE Science open-ended answer has three parts: say what happens, give the science reason using the correct keyword, then link that reason back to the situation in the question. If your child writes only the first part, the answer sounds sensible but usually earns half the marks or less. Teaching the other two parts is the fastest fix I know for Booklet B.

Why most open-ended answers stop too early

Children are good at noticing what happens. "The water level went down." "The handle got hot." That is the observation, and it is already in the question, so it rarely earns a mark on its own. The marks sit in the explanation, and the explanation has to be written the way the mark scheme expects. This is why a child who truly understands the topic can still leave marks on the table.

Part 1: State what happens, using the question's own words

Start by answering the exact thing asked. If the question says "Explain why the water level in beaker A decreased", the first line talks about the water level in beaker A. This keeps the answer on the question and stops the child from writing out everything they know about the topic.

Part 2: Give the reason with the science keyword

This is the mark-earning part. Here the child names the process or property in correct PSLE language, for example evaporation, condensation, conduction, good conductor, poor conductor, or gains heat and loses heat. A loose phrase such as "the water went away" usually cannot be matched against the mark scheme. A keyword can.

Part 3: Link the reason back to this situation

Many children recite the definition and stop. The link is the sentence that connects the reason to the particular container, object or experiment in the question. It answers "so what happened here?" and it is the part a marker looks for when an answer is asked to be explained, not just recalled.

Example: the same answer, built in three parts

Question (2 marks): Two identical beakers contain the same amount of water. Beaker A is left uncovered and beaker B is covered with a lid. After two days, the water level in A is lower than in B. Explain why.

Part 1, what happens: The water level in beaker A decreased because the water in A changed from liquid to water vapour.

Part 2, keyword reason: This process is called evaporation.

Part 3, link: The water vapour escaped into the air from the uncovered beaker A, but in beaker B the lid stopped the water vapour from escaping.

Notice that nothing is added about the whole water cycle. Each sentence answers this question about these two beakers.

How to practise it at home in ten minutes

Choose one marked open-ended question your child lost marks on. Ask them to underline the three parts in the model answer, then rewrite their own answer using the same three parts. Do this with three or four questions a week rather than one long session. Over time the structure becomes automatic, and that is when the lost marks start to come back.

One caution: this structure is a thinking tool, not a script to be copied word for word. Each question still needs the right keyword for its topic, and a few topics, such as fair test questions, ask for different information. Those are covered in separate posts.

PSLE Science answering technique: common questions

How many parts should a PSLE Science open-ended answer have? +

It depends on the marks. As a safe rule, plan one clear idea per mark. The 3-part structure (what happens, the keyword reason, the link back to the question) is a checklist to make sure each idea is complete, not a fixed number of sentences.

Should my child write in full sentences? +

Clear, short sentences are the safest. They should be complete enough that the marker can see the cause and the effect, and specific enough to use the correct science words. Long paragraphs are not needed.

What if my child does not remember the exact keyword? +

The mark scheme looks for the science idea in correct terms, so a vague everyday phrase may not earn the mark. Learn the keyword together with each topic, and let the child practise writing it from memory.

At what age should we start practising this? +

From Primary 5 is a sensible start, so the habit is already built before the P6 year. The free open-ended guide on this site shows the same idea with more worked examples.
